# What ISS air systems can and cannot tell us about fire location

This is a source review for Ignos, not a location-specific fire forecast. It separates the contained BASS-II experiment from station cabin conditions. The public NASA drawings below are system and module schematics, not a current, dimensioned blueprint or a map of measured airflow at every possible ignition site.

## Pressure, hatches, and oxygen

NASA describes the ISS cabin as approximately **14.7 psia and 21% oxygen**. Atmosphere Control and Supply maintains pressure and the oxygen and nitrogen mix. Cabin pressure and oxygen fraction are separate inputs: oxygen partial pressure is total pressure multiplied by oxygen fraction. A hatch opening does not by itself imply a known cabin pressure change. NASA places manual pressure-equalization valves on hatches so pressure can be balanced before opening; isolation or a leak can instead leave compartments with different conditions. An airlock is a controlled exception with its own depressurization operations. Sources: [NASA ISS operations book, chapter 19, p. 339](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/iss-operating_an_outpost-tagged.pdf), [NASA ISS Familiarization, section 6.3.1, pp. 6-2 to 6-5](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250005232/downloads/International%20Space%20Station%20Familiarization.pdf), and [NASA ECLSS atmosphere study, section 6.1, p. 13](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260003925/downloads/20260003925.pdf).

The [NASA ECLSS atmosphere study, section 6.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260003925/downloads/20260003925.pdf) cites an ISS cabin design range of **13.9 to 15.2 psia** and oxygen partial pressure of **2.83 to 3.35 psia**. These are operating design bounds, not a measured pressure trace for the BASS-II burns. They cannot establish whether a particular hatch cycle changed a particular flame.

Changing pressure can change gas density, transport, cooling, and material flammability. Maintaining oxygen partial pressure while lowering total pressure often requires a higher oxygen fraction; that fraction is itself a flammability concern. A single oxygen percentage is therefore insufficient to transfer a burn threshold between atmospheres. This is a physical inference supported by the [NASA ECLSS atmosphere study, sections 3 and 6.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260003925/downloads/20260003925.pdf) and NASA's [pressure and flammability research record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20070005041). It does not quantify the sign or size of a change for a specific Ignos test.

## Ventilation and location

NASA distinguishes **rack**, **intramodule**, and **intermodule** circulation. Equipment fans move air within racks. Cabin fans and Common Cabin Air Assemblies circulate it within a module. Intermodule Ventilation fans, valves, ducts, and sometimes open hatches connect modules. The [ISS Familiarization, section 6.3.3, pp. 6-9 to 6-13](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250005232/downloads/International%20Space%20Station%20Familiarization.pdf) shows this hierarchy. The [NASA ISS operations book, chapter 19, p. 339, Fig. 4](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/iss-operating_an_outpost-tagged.pdf) supplies a public Node 2 duct and valve schematic and says intermodule ventilation can recirculate station air in about **2 to 3 hours**. That whole-station turnover time is not a local flame-facing air velocity.

The public module schematics identify Destiny Laboratory, Node 1, Node 2, the airlock, and connections to other modules. They show where ducts, pressure controls, and hatches exist, but not a verified airflow vector at an arbitrary cabin coordinate. Fire or smoke near a rack intake, in the open cabin, at a hatch, or inside an isolated module can experience different forced-flow and smoke-transport conditions. The exact difference needs measured fan and valve states, hatch state, module geometry, and local flow. NASA's [2021 fire-safety presentation, section "Fire Detection in Lunar-G"](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210021318/downloads/Tech%20Dev%20for%20Spacecraft%20Fire%20Safety_Location_10-7-2021.pdf) illustrates that detector reach depends on smoke transport and ventilation near the source. This is a reason to label locations, not a basis for a cabin hazard map.

For the public layout, NASA's [ISS operations book, chapter 3, p. 48](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/iss-operating_an_outpost-tagged.pdf) describes Node 2 as a pressure shell with radial and axial ports and shows its assembly, while chapter 19 shows the ventilation paths. These are suitable for a labeled compartment and duct diagram. They do not specify current rack configurations, open-hatch states, or local flow fields, so an image-based Ignos rate must not be assigned to a station coordinate from that drawing alone.

NASA's [Fire Protection technical brief, pp. 2-3](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-008-fire-protection.pdf) says the ISS response terminates ventilation in the affected module **to slow the spread of fire**, shuts intermodule ventilation and closes powered valves, then uses masks, power removal, extinguishers, and isolation as needed. It also notes that some burning plastics can generate flame-stimulating flow through boiling and vapor-jet ejection. It does not say that switching off fans alone always extinguishes a fire. Ignos should say "ventilation shutdown is part of the station response" or "reduces forced airflow and slows spread". The stronger line "fans off starves every station fire" is not established by this source or by the BASS-II rates.

An independent NASA ISS experiment sharpens that limit. The [ACME Burning Rate Emulator report, section 3.3, p. 7](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210021781/downloads/JASMAC%20ACME%20paper%2020210921.pdf) reports that low-momentum microgravity flames can burn for minutes without imposed airflow at elevated oxygen, while the tested flames self-extinguished below roughly 25% oxygen. These were **gas-fed burner flames emulating condensed fuels**, not BASS-II PMMA rods or an open-cabin fire. They establish that "fan off" alone is not a universal extinction condition; they do not set an Ignos rod or cabin threshold.

## What the BASS-II images actually measure

BASS-II burned small solid samples in a **flow duct inside the Microgravity Science Glovebox in Destiny Laboratory**, not in an open cabin. Its fan imposed the **oxidizer airflow** past the rod. The glovebox working volume was sometimes diluted with nitrogen to set oxygen concentration. NASA recorded oxygen before and after burns; oxygen fell during each burn while combustion products rose. The fan's airspeed sensor had an approximately **1-minute** response time. Sources: [NASA BASS-II Summary Report, pp. 3-6](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011385/downloads/TM-20210011385.pdf), especially Fig. 1.1 and section 1.2.

For example, the frozen B16 and B19 rows report **18.2%** and **17.5%** oxygen in the experiment, below the station's nominal cabin fraction. Source: `data/derived/spread_rates.csv`, `o2_pct` column, with the published conditions in Link et al. (2018), Supplement Table S1, p. 1. Those values describe the test atmosphere in the glovebox, not the air in every ISS cabin.

The `flow_cm_s` in Link Table S1 and `data/derived/spread_rates.csv` is air or oxidizer flow past the PMMA rod. It is **not a supplied fuel-flow rate**. The rod is the solid fuel; heat-driven pyrolysis and mass loss supply its vapor. NASA's report says the samples were returned and evaluated for mass loss, and it lists fuel type, thickness, geometry, oxygen, and ventilation flow as experimental variables. A hypothetical gas leak or liquid-fed fire would need fuel-flow and leak data from a different source. [NASA BASS-II Summary Report, pp. 2-5](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011385/downloads/TM-20210011385.pdf).

This matters for regression. A spread-rate slope within one flow step assumes sufficiently steady oxidizer flow, oxygen mix, fuel surface, and image scale during the fitted interval. The frozen tracker already drops the first part of a step because of the sensor response and flow-change transient. It cannot remove unrecorded oxygen depletion, pressure drift, or changes in fuel geometry. NASA recorded initial and final gas concentrations, but Ignos has not joined synchronized per-photo pressure, oxygen, gas-flow, and mass-loss series to the edge tracks. An apparent edge-speed change can therefore be a tracker error, a changing combustion state, or both. The [BASS-II Summary Report, pp. 3-6](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011385/downloads/TM-20210011385.pdf) supports these variable definitions; the missing joined series is a statement about the current Ignos data contract.

## A defensible next data contract

For any future location-aware analysis, record `module`, `rack_or_open_cabin`, `hatch_state`, `intermodule_valve_state`, `local_fan_state`, `pressure_psia`, `oxygen_pct`, `oxygen_partial_pressure_psia`, `fuel_material`, `fuel_geometry`, `fuel_supply_type`, `fuel_mass_loss_or_feed_rate`, and synchronized timestamps. Attach a source and uncertainty to each value. Mark unknown values unknown. Use an ISS module schematic to name compartments and flow paths; do not assign a fire threshold or evacuation route to a coordinate until measured local flow and combustion data support it. This is a proposed schema, not an assertion that NASA publishes all those data.

### Coordinate and API boundary for Ignos

An API record needs a `location_frame` that names a specific module-fixed coordinate system before `x_m`, `y_m`, or `z_m` can have meaning. Each position needs its own measured source and uncertainty. A module name or a diagram pixel is not a three-dimensional position. A pressure or airflow reading also needs a UTC timestamp, sensor identity, calibration, and the hatch and fan configuration at that time. The API should keep `oxidizer_flow_cm_s` separate from `fuel_feed_g_s`; neither may be inferred from the other. Compute `oxygen_partial_pressure_psia = pressure_psia * oxygen_pct / 100` only when both inputs describe the same enclosure and time.

For the existing B16 low-flow step, the only defensible location fields are `module = Destiny Laboratory` and `enclosure = Microgravity Science Glovebox flow duct`. Its `location_frame`, `x_m`, `y_m`, `z_m`, cabin pressure, local cabin airflow vector, and fuel-feed rate are **unknown**, not zero. `data/derived/spread_rates.csv` reports `flow_cm_s = 0.72` as experimental oxidizer flow, `o2_pct = 18.2` as the test atmosphere, and `rate_mm_s = 0.0576` as our image-derived spread rate. The [BASS-II Summary Report, Fig. 1.1 and section 1.2](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210011385/downloads/TM-20210011385.pdf) locates the experiment and defines its flow. No current Ignos API endpoint accepts or verifies location telemetry. A location question should therefore return **location-specific risk not determined from these data**, with those source limits, until measured records are ingested and validated.
