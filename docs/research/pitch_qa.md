## 1. "Why should we trust your numbers?"
- "We didn't take NASA's numbers on faith, and we didn't fit ours to them. We measured flame speed from NASA's own raw space-station photos, PSI-25 BASS-II, and only then compared."
- "Three things were measured independently of NASA's rates. The scale comes from the rod's own width in the photos: 34.83 px/mm, against 34 in the paper. The flow-step times come from the fan setting and clock printed on NASA's test videos. And the fitting rule was frozen before the final numbers."
- "Result: 8 low-flow segments from 4 tests, median error 6.8%, all within 15%, and mean signed error +1.1%." If asked how sure: "With 8 segments, the median's 95% interval is about 2 to 13%. The bias interval includes zero, so we did not detect a mean bias." Source: `data/derived/spread_rates.csv`, `pipeline/METHOD.md`, against Link et al. 2018, Sci. Rep. 8:120, Supplement Table S1, p. 1.
- "A second, independent open-source flame tracker gets the same answer within 2% on the test we checked." (`docs/research/flametracker_crosscheck.md`)
- "Where it fails, we show it. Some fast-flow steps don't match, and we flag them in the data and on the chart instead of hiding them. We found out why: they fall in the flame's bright early phase, where a brightness threshold locks onto the core. A blue-light edge recovers two of those tests to within about 10%, and a fifth test whose fast step falls in the dim phase matches within 6%. A sixth test mostly fails, and it's in the data too."
- If pressed on code: "The automated tracker suite includes a moving-bar test within 1% and a more realistic flame benchmark that shows where the method fails. A code review with an independent re-check found 3 bugs before submission, and we fixed them."

## 2. "Why is the Moon different?"
- "In orbit, buoyancy is very weak. Ending forced ventilation in the affected module is an early ISS fire response that NASA says slows spread. It is one part of a response that also includes power removal, extinguishers and isolation. Our contained BASS-II measurements show the effect of imposed flow on a PMMA rod; they do not prove that fans off extinguishes every station fire." ([NASA fire protection brief, pp. 2-3](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-008-fire-protection.pdf); `docs/research/accuracy/station_environment.md`.)
- "On the Moon, gravity is a sixth of Earth's, but it isn't zero. Hot air still rises, so the air keeps moving even with the fans off. Nobody has measured how fast, and we never put a number on it."
- "NASA's lunar-gravity paper (Urban, Olson, Dietrich et al., ICES-2025-392, p. 9) says that in some figures the lunar buoyant limit is below the Earth extrapolation, contrary to expected behavior. Our largest clearly positive gap is 2.7 oxygen points, with a 95% range of 1.8 to 3.7, in the figure **captioned** P-213. The plotted points in Figures 7 to 9 appear inconsistent with their material labels and Table 2, so we do not attribute that result to the figure labelled P-213 as a settled fact. These tests do not establish whether the full station fire response transfers to a habitat." Numbers: `web/src/data/moongap.json` (`ranking`, caveats); a team relay review (15 Nov).
- "Caveat we say out loud: those lunar tests were about 5-second drop-tower centrifuge runs, and our zero-flow value is an extrapolation."
- Two sentences from NASA-funded work that carry the whole argument, both verbatim from Link et al. 2018 (Sci. Rep. 8:120, Results, text under Fig. 6; open access, text in `docs/sources/Link2018_SciRep_main.txt`):
  - In orbit: "No flame spread was observed at flow velocities less than 0.6 cm/s." (0.64 cm rods at 18.2% and 17.5% O2.)
  - With gravity: "the low-flow extinction of the flame is not observed in normal gravity because the buoyant flow always presents." (sic)
  - Our reading, not the paper's: the Moon has gravity too, so some buoyant flow is there. Nobody has measured how much, and we never put a number on it.

## Likely follow-ups
- **"Why only 4 tests?"** "The headline uses every step below 1 cm/s that passes our automatic confidence check, and those all sit in four tests. One more low-flow step, B12 at 0.82 cm/s, fails that check and misses by 93%; the check never looks at NASA's numbers, and that step is in the data and on the chart. We measured two more tests at higher flow, B13b and B13a, and we report them, mostly as failures. Four others were dropped because their flow steps can't be read from NASA's video." (B9, B22, B10 and C25; see METHOD.md. Link's data "were not systematically taken".)
- **"Did you try to fix the fast-flow failures?"** "Yes, and we report that it didn't work. The obvious upgrade fuses the blue-light edge with the brightness edge. Before measuring any new test, we wrote down the rule for adopting it. On the held-out tests its median error was 11.0% against 8.7% for the original, from only 3 steps, so we kept the original for every number." (`pipeline/PREREGISTRATION.md`)
- **"Why not closer than 6.8%?"** "NASA doesn't publish an uncertainty for its own spread rates, so we can't say how close is possible. With our own error bars and NASA's numbers taken as exact, 6 of the 8 steps agree within two standard deviations. And these are the same burns NASA measured, so it's a consistency check, not independent proof." Don't claim that NASA's oxygen uncertainty explains the gap: it doesn't, because both measurements are of the same flame. (`docs/research/accuracy/analysis.json`, `uncertainty_floor.md`)
- **"Did you check NASA's own records?"** "Yes. NASA's test table logs oxygen before and after every burn, and it fell 0.3 to 1.6 points in each of our six tests (0.2 to 1.7 across the whole table, leaving out one bad reading for B22). So a test labelled 18.2% oxygen ran its slow-airflow steps nearer 17.5%. That doesn't change our measurements, but anyone comparing these flames with a model or a habitat should know the labels are starting values. We checked that the published oxygen trend still holds when you correct for it, and it does." (NASA PSI-25 experimental table, calibrated initial and final O2; Olson, Ferkul et al., ICES-2015-196, p. 4: "The O2 decreased during each burn." Details: `docs/research/accuracy/uncertainty_floor.md`.)
